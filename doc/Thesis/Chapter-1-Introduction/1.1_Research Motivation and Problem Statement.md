## _A. Research Objectives_

- `Vector Space Analysis`
Vector space analysis is a computational approach in bioinformatics that evaluates gene expression diversity across conditions, such as tissues, species, or developmental stages. By representing gene expression levels as vectors in a multidimensional space, this method helps uncover genetic mechanisms that drive biological structures and functions.

- `Project Goal`
- In this project the goal is to reproduce the vector space analyses as done in
the _Cardamine hirsuta_ genome project [1]. The R-Code required to do so shall
be isolated and made usabel with _any_ data. Next, this code shall be used to
analyse public open access data of model species to evaluate whether the method
works for other data-sets as well.


- `Research Question / Hypothesis`
> How does the mean distance between orthologous genes compare to that of paralogous genes, 
> and what implications does this have for understanding evolutionary relationships and 
> functional divergence in gene families?

• In the motivation include the questions:
◦ What happens to gene expression after duplication?
◦ What happens to gene expression after speciation?

[1]

## _B. Problem Definition_

- The primary challenge in the field lies in accurately distinguishing between orthologs and paralogs, particularly when predicting orthologous relationships

- Validation of predicted orthologous relationships is hindered by the absence of a universal ground truth

- The complexity of orthology prediction increases significantly with large-scale analyses, requiring sophisticated computational approaches

- Establishing clear boundaries between different types of homologous relationships remains a persistent challenge in the field

- The computational demands of orthology prediction are substantial:

    - New genomes are released weekly (Mukherjee et al. 2019)
    - Computational complexity scales at least linearly, often quadratically, with the number of available genomes
    - Continuous data integration and standardization efforts are essential to manage the rapidly growing volume of genomic data

- Accurate orthology inference is fundamental to many fields of biology, with orthologous gene alignments serving as the foundation for systematic and evolutionary studies

- The implications extend to numerous downstream analyses, including gene functional predictions and biological network interpretation

- Since the advent of genome comparison, a central question has emerged: how should we define corresponding genes across different species? While it may seem intuitive to think of these as "the same" genes in different species, this simplification can be misleading

[2]


* **Fundamental Importance**

  - Orthologs and paralogs represent fundamentally different types of homologous genes (vertical descent vs. duplication), making their distinction key to evolutionary genomics

* **Practical Applications**
  - Clear distinction is critical for robust evolutionary classification and reliable functional annotation of newly sequenced genomes

## _C. Conceptual Framework_

- **Expression Comparison**: Distinguishes orthologs from paralogs by analyzing expression similarity; aids in functional predictions, evolutionary studies, and genome annotation.  
- **Comparative Analysis**: Compares gene sequences and features across species to reconstruct evolutionary histories and identify conserved elements.  
- **Scientific Value**: Provides functional validation, insights into gene evolution, and supports genomic annotation efforts.  
[4]


- `Expression Vector Space`

  * **Expression Vector Space Purpose**

        - Represents gene expression as numerical vectors where:
          - Each dimension corresponds to a different condition (tissue/time point)
          - Vector coordinates represent expression levels
          - Distances between vectors indicate functional similarity
          - Patterns reveal evolutionary and functional relationships

      * **Dimensionality**
        - Each tissue/condition represents one dimension
        - Total dimensions = number of analyzed conditions
        - Example: 10 tissues = 10-dimensional space
        - Allows multi-tissue comparison simultaneously

      * **Vector Construction**
        - Each gene becomes a point in multi-dimensional space
        - Expression values are normalized (RPKM/TPM)
        - Coordinates determined by expression levels
        - Normalization ensures fair comparisons

      * **Expression Analysis & Interpretation**
        - Quantitative Analysis:
          - Calculates distances between mean expression vectors
          - Measures gene activity variation across conditions
          - Provides robust comparison metrics

      * **Biological Meaning**
        - Greater distances suggest functional divergence
        - Similar vectors indicate conserved regulation
        - Vector orientation reveals tissue-specific adaptations

      * **Key Applications**
        - Comparing gene expression patterns quantitatively
        - Measuring functional divergence between genes
        - Analyzing evolutionary relationships
        - Identifying similar gene behaviors across conditions

  - **Gene_Expression:**

      * **Gene Expression Definition**
        - Process of converting genetic information into functional products
        - Primarily results in protein production
        - Measurable through RNA levels in cells
        - Key indicator of gene function and activity

      * **High Expression States**
        - Abundant RNA production
        - Active gene transcription
        - High levels of protein synthesis
        - Strong gene activity in specific tissue/condition

      * **Low Expression States**
        - Minimal RNA production
        - Repressed gene transcription
        - Little to no protein synthesis
        - Gene inactivity or reduced importance

      * **Expression Variability**
        - Changes across different tissues
        - Varies during developmental stages
        - Responds to environmental conditions
        - Adapts to cellular states (stress, disease)

[2]


- **Homology**


  * **Fundamental Definitions**

        - Orthologs: Genes in different species that evolved from a common ancestral gene through speciation
        - Paralogs: Genes in the same species that evolved from a common ancestral gene through duplication

  * **Key Characteristics**

        - Orthologs:
          - Found in different species
          - Typically maintain similar biological functions
          - Result from speciation events
          - Often show higher functional conservation
        
        - Paralogs:
          - Found within the same species
          - May develop new or specialized functions
          - Result from gene duplication events
          - Often show more functional divergence

        - Orthology and paralogy are evolutionary relationships linking
          homologous genes which diverged via speciation or duplica-
          tion events, respectively (Fitch 1970). [1]


    ### **Homology Types**


    - **Con_orthologs**:  
      Genes that are orthologous between species and share a direct evolutionary relationship due to speciation.

    - **In_paralogs**:  
      Paralogous genes resulting from lineage-specific duplications after a speciation event, defined relative to the event.

    - **Out_paralogs**:  
      Paralogous genes arising from duplications that occurred before a speciation event, defined relative to the event.

    - **Special_in_paralogs**:  
      A specific subset of inparalogs, often associated with unique functions or adaptations within a lineage.

    - **Special_out_paralogs**:  
      A distinct subset of outparalogs that may provide insights into ancient duplication events or evolutionary trends.

[4]
