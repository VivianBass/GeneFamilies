## 1.1. **Research Motivation and Problem Statement**

### _A. Research Objectives_

- **Vector Space Analysis**  
Vector space analysis is a powerful computational tool in bioinformatics that quantifies and visualizes gene expression diversity across various biological conditions, such as tissue types, developmental stages, or environmental factors. By representing gene expression levels as vectors in a multidimensional space, this method allows for the systematic exploration of genetic mechanisms that drive the development of biological traits and functions. This approach provides a comprehensive view of how gene expression evolves and diversifies within and across species.

- **Project Goal**  
The primary objective of this project is to reproduce the vector space analyses implemented in the *Cardamine hirsuta* genome project ([DOI: 10.1038/nplants.2016.167](https://doi.org/10.1038/nplants.2016.167)). The focus will be on extracting the R-code used for vector space analysis and adapting it to be applicable to any dataset. Once the code is generalized, it will be applied to analyze publicly available, open-access data from model species to test whether this method can be extended and applied to a wider range of datasets, enhancing its robustness and utility in comparative genomics.

- **Research Question / Hypothesis**  
  > How does the mean distance between orthologous genes compare to that of paralogous genes, and what implications does this have for understanding evolutionary relationships and functional divergence in gene families?

- **Key Evolutionary Questions**

1. **What happens to gene expression after duplication?**  
   Gene duplication events give rise to paralogs, which evolve independently from one another. This process introduces genetic redundancy, leading to the possibility of:
   - **Subfunctionalization**: The division of the ancestral function between the duplicated copies.
   - **Neofunctionalization**: The evolution of a novel function in one of the duplicated genes.
   - **Expression Divergence**: Changes in the regulation of gene expression across different tissues or environmental conditions.  
   Understanding these patterns is essential for revealing the mechanisms of functional innovation, the evolution of regulatory networks, and the preservation or loss of duplicated genes.

2. **What happens to gene expression after speciation?**  
   After speciation, orthologous genes in different species may face distinct evolutionary pressures, including:
   - **Selective Constraints**: The need to maintain essential functions.
   - **Species-Specific Adaptation**: The requirement for new or modified functions in each species.
   - **Tissue-Specific Regulation**: Changes in how genes are expressed in different tissue types across species.
   These pressures may lead to variations in expression patterns, raising questions about the conservation of gene expression across species, the role of regulatory evolution in speciation, and the balance between functional conservation and adaptation.

These questions are fundamental to understanding the evolutionary forces that shape gene function and expression. The vector space analysis approach offers a quantitative framework to systematically compare gene expression divergence and to explore the evolutionary relationships between orthologous and paralogous gene families. It allows us to measure the extent of functional divergence and provides insight into regulatory innovations that occur during evolution.

### _B. Problem Definition_

The primary challenge in the field of evolutionary genomics is distinguishing between orthologs and paralogs, particularly when predicting orthologous relationships across different species. This distinction is crucial because orthologous genes, which arise from speciation, typically retain more functional similarity across species, whereas paralogs, which result from gene duplication events, can evolve divergent functions within the same species. Accurate orthology inference is foundational for understanding evolutionary histories and for predicting gene function across species.

However, several challenges complicate orthology prediction:
- **Lack of a Universal Ground Truth**: There is no definitive reference for orthologous relationships, making it difficult to validate predictions.  
- **Complexity in Large-Scale Analyses**: The scale of genomic data and the increasing number of sequenced genomes require sophisticated computational methods to accurately predict orthologs and paralogs.  
- **Uncertainty in Homology Definitions**: There is ongoing debate about how to define homologous genes across species, as the evolutionary processes that give rise to these relationships are complex and varied.

Furthermore, the computational demands of orthology prediction are substantial:
- With new genomes being sequenced regularly (Mukherjee et al., 2019), the volume of data is growing rapidly, and the computational complexity often scales with the number of genomes analyzed, requiring more advanced algorithms and computational power.
- The increasing volume and diversity of genomic data require continuous efforts in data integration, standardization, and validation to ensure the reliability of orthology predictions across large-scale datasets.

The ability to accurately infer orthologous relationships is critical not only for understanding evolutionary processes but also for practical applications, such as annotating newly sequenced genomes, predicting gene functions, and interpreting biological networks. As a result, improving orthology prediction methods remains a central challenge in genomics.

### _C. Conceptual Framework_

- **Expression Comparison**  
The primary focus of this study is to compare the expression of orthologous and paralogous genes to better understand their functional similarities and differences. By analyzing gene expression profiles, we can infer the degree of functional conservation or divergence between these gene types. Expression comparison also provides insights into the role of gene regulation in maintaining or diversifying gene functions across species and tissues.

- **Comparative Analysis**  
This involves comparing gene sequences, gene families, and expression data across species to reconstruct evolutionary histories and identify conserved elements. By examining the genetic and functional divergence of orthologs and paralogs, we can gain insights into how these genes evolve and adapt over time, helping to clarify the mechanisms driving evolutionary processes.

- **Scientific Value**  
Comparative gene expression analysis is invaluable for functional predictions, evolutionary studies, and genome annotation. It provides a robust framework for identifying conserved genetic elements, assessing functional divergence, and exploring the evolutionary forces shaping gene function across species.

#### **Expression Vector Space**

- **Expression Vector Space Purpose**  
Gene expression is represented as numerical vectors where each dimension corresponds to a different biological condition (e.g., tissue type, developmental stage, or environmental condition). The coordinates of the vector reflect gene expression levels under these conditions. The distance between vectors indicates the degree of functional similarity, helping to identify genes with similar expression profiles and functional roles across conditions.

- **Dimensionality**  
Each biological condition (e.g., different tissues or time points) constitutes one dimension in the vector space. For example, if 10 tissues are analyzed, the vector space would have 10 dimensions, allowing for a simultaneous comparison of gene expression across multiple conditions.

- **Vector Construction**  
Each gene is represented as a point in this multidimensional space, with expression values normalized (e.g., RPKM or TPM) to ensure fair comparisons across different genes and conditions. These vectors can then be analyzed to quantify gene activity and divergence, offering insights into functional and regulatory evolution.

- **Expression Analysis & Interpretation**  
By calculating distances between expression vectors, we can measure the variability of gene activity across conditions. Genes with similar vectors are assumed to have conserved functions, while greater distances suggest functional divergence. This approach allows for a detailed exploration of gene regulation and expression patterns across species, tissues, and conditions.

- **Biological Meaning**  
Vector space analysis provides a quantitative measure of gene expression divergence, allowing us to distinguish between conserved regulatory mechanisms and those that have evolved to support species- or tissue-specific functions. The analysis can reveal cases where functional divergence occurs and highlight evolutionary innovations that drive adaptation.

#### **Gene Expression**

- **Gene Expression Definition**  
Gene expression is the process by which genetic information is used to produce functional gene products, typically proteins. Gene expression can be measured by quantifying RNA levels in cells, providing insights into the activity and functional roles of genes.

- **High Expression States**  
Genes that are highly expressed produce abundant RNA and are typically involved in active cellular processes. These genes may be critical for specific biological functions, such as tissue development or response to environmental stimuli.

- **Low Expression States**  
Genes with low expression levels produce minimal RNA and are typically inactive or expressed only under specific conditions. Low expression can indicate that the gene is not needed under normal conditions or that it is tightly regulated.

- **Expression Variability**  
Gene expression varies across different tissues, developmental stages, and environmental conditions. Understanding this variability is key to understanding how genes function in different biological contexts and how gene regulation evolves in response to external and internal factors.

#### **Homology**

- **Fundamental Definitions**
  - **Orthologs**: Genes in different species that share a common ancestral gene and were formed through speciation events. Orthologs generally maintain similar functions across species.
  - **Paralogs**: Genes within the same species that arise from gene duplication events. Paralogs may evolve new or specialized functions over time.

- **Key Characteristics**
  - **Orthologs** are typically more functionally conserved because they are the result of speciation events, which preserve essential functions across species.
  - **Paralogs** may exhibit greater functional divergence due to gene duplication, leading to new or specialized functions within a species.

    ### **Homology Types**


    - **Con_orthologs**:  
      Genes that are orthologous between species and share a direct evolutionary relationship due to speciation.

    - **In_paralogs**:  
      Paralogous genes resulting from lineage-specific duplications after a speciation event, defined relative to the event.

    - **Out_paralogs**:  
      Paralogous genes arising from duplications that occurred before a speciation event, defined relative to the event.

    - **Special_in_paralogs**:  
      A specific subset of inparalogs, often associated with unique functions or adaptations within a lineage.

    - **Special_out_paralogs**:  
      A distinct subset of outparalogs that may provide insights into ancient duplication events or evolutionary trends.
