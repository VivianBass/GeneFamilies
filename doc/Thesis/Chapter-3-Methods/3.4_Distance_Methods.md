

## Overview of Distance Metrics

1. **Euclidean Distance**: Measures direct spatial distance between expression vectors
2. **Cosine Angle**: Captures directional similarity between expression patterns
3. **Angle to Diagonal**: Quantifies tissue specificity and expression uniformity


## **Euclidean Distances**

- _Formula:_

    $$
    d_{euclidean}(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - y_i)^2}
    $$ (1)

- _Purpose:_ 

    - **Euclidean Distance Calculation**: Measures the straight-line distance between two points in n-dimensional space, calculated as the square root of the sum of squared differences.

    - **All-vs-All Comparison**: Distances are computed for all possible gene pairs within gene-family clusters.

    - **Orthogroup Cluster**: Groups of genes classified by Orthofinder, with pairwise distances calculated within each cluster.

    - **Expression Profiles**: Each gene has an associated expression profile (gene expression counts), which is compared pairwise within the cluster.

    - **Purpose**: To assess similarities or differences in gene activity, highlighting functional diversification within gene-family clusters.

    - **Small Distances**: Indicate similar gene expression patterns, suggesting similar gene activity.

    - **Large Distances**: Indicate divergent expression patterns, suggesting functional differentiation.

    - **Output**: A distance matrix representing pairwise expression comparisons between genes within a cluster.

    - **Example**: For a cluster with 161 genes, 12,880 pairwise distances are calculated using the formula: `n * (n-1) / 2`, where n is the number of genes.



### **Angle Distances (Cosine Similarity)**

- **Cosine Similarity Formula**:  

  The cosine similarity between two vectors \( \mathbf{x} \) and \( \mathbf{y} \) is calculated using the formula:
  
  $$
  \cos(\theta) = \frac{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\| \|\mathbf{y}\|}
  $$

  Where \( \mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y} \) is the dot product of the vectors, and \( \|\mathbf{x}\| \) and \( \|\mathbf{y}\| \) are their magnitudes.

- **Angle Calculation**:  

  To calculate the angle \( \theta \) between two vectors, use:
  
  $$
  \theta = \arccos\left( \frac{\mathbf{x} \cdot \mathbf{y}}{\|\mathbf{x}\| \|\mathbf{y}\|} \right)
  $$

- **Interpretation of Angle Distances**:  

  - **Cosine similarity** measures the orientation (not magnitude) of vectors in a vector space, i.e., how similar their patterns are.
  
  - The result ranges from **-1** to **1**:
    - **1**: Vectors point in the same direction (identical expression patterns).
    - **0**: Vectors are perpendicular (no relationship between expression patterns).
    - **-1**: Vectors point in opposite directions (inverse expression patterns).

- **Example**:  
  For gene expression vectors:
  - Gene 1 = [2, 3, 1, 0]
  - Gene 2 = [4, 6, 2, 0]
  
  Even though Gene 2 has higher expression values, the cosine similarity will be close to **1** because the expression patterns are the same.

### **Why Use Cosine Distance Over Euclidean Distance?**

- **Focus on Pattern, Not Magnitude**:  
  Cosine similarity measures the similarity in expression patterns, regardless of the absolute magnitude of expression levels. This makes it ideal for comparing gene activity profiles where the relative pattern is more important than the raw expression values.

- **Handling Scale Differences**:  
  Cosine similarity is less sensitive to differences in scale (e.g., one gene may have higher expression levels than another, but their patterns could still be similar). In contrast, Euclidean distance is heavily influenced by scale, which can distort comparisons.

- **Effective for High-Dimensional Data**:  
  In high-dimensional spaces, like gene expression profiles across many conditions, cosine similarity is more robust at capturing similarities in patterns, especially when some values are zero or missing.

## **Angle to Diagonal**

The **Angle to Diagonal** is calculated to assess the *tissue versatility* of a gene, which refers to the extent to which the gene's expression profile is consistent across different tissues.

### Formula:

The cosine of the angle (\(\theta\)) between a gene's expression vector \(\mathbf{x}\) and a diagonal vector \(\mathbf{d}\) (representing uniform expression across tissues) is calculated using the following formula:

$$
\cos(\theta) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i d_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2}}
$$

Where:
- \(x_i\) is the expression level of the gene in tissue \(i\).
- \(d_i\) represents the components of the diagonal vector, which in this case is equal to 1 for each tissue (representing uniform expression across tissues).

### Normalization:

To ensure comparability across different numbers of tissues, the cosine value is normalized by dividing by \(\sqrt{2}\):

$$
\text{angle\_diag} = \frac{\cos(\theta)}{\sqrt{2}}
$$

This normalization accounts for the fact that the diagonal vector has a length of \(\sqrt{n}\), where \(n\) is the number of tissues. It standardizes the angle to make it consistent across datasets with different numbers of tissues.

### Purpose:

1. **Tissue Versatility**:  
   The angle to the diagonal measures the **versatility** of gene expression across tissues. A smaller angle indicates that the gene is more evenly expressed across tissues (i.e., more versatile), while a larger angle suggests that the gene is more tissue-specific (i.e., expressed strongly in some tissues and weakly in others).

2. **Interpretation**:  
   - **Small angle**: Indicates uniform expression across tissues, meaning the gene has broad tissue versatility.
   - **Large angle**: Indicates tissue-specific expression, meaning the gene is active only in certain tissues and not in others.

### Why Calculate Angle to Diagonal?

- **Tissue Versatility**: By calculating the angle to the diagonal, we can quantify how versatile a gene is across tissues. Genes that maintain similar expression patterns across tissues are considered to have higher tissue versatility.
  
- **Comparative Analysis**: This measure allows for comparison of gene expression patterns across a wide range of genes, helping to identify those that show broad or specific expression profiles. It provides insight into the functional roles of genes in different biological contexts, highlighting whether they play general or tissue-specific roles.
